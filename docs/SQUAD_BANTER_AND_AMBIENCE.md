# Squad Banter, Environmental Scares and Dark-Comedy Tone Guide

**Applies to:** the complete SPECIAL OPS / Operation Iron Veil campaign  
**Status:** canonical tone and implementation guide  
**Primary references:** `docs/CAMPAIGN_STORY_BIBLE.md`, `docs/CAMPAIGN_AND_CINEMATICS.md`

## 1. The tone we are aiming for

SPECIAL OPS should feel like a serious, cinematic military operation being experienced by a squad of capable people who are also tired, human, occasionally immature and very capable of being startled by something stupid.

The balance is:

- **The mission is serious.** The Northline crisis, Venn's disappearance and Kade's conspiracy are real stakes inside the fictional story.
- **The squad is funny.** The operators tease one another, swear when startled, complain about uncomfortable places, make dry observations and occasionally lose their composure.
- **The environment can be unsettling.** Forest calls, distant howls, metallic groans, ventilation, strange echoes and uncertain movement can make the team pause and listen.
- **The payoff is often mundane.** A frightening sound turns out to be a loose sign, a vent, an animal in the distance or the vehicle cooling down. This sets up the V-01 dummy reveal.
- **The game is not a horror game.** No supernatural explanation, gore, monster combat, relentless jump scares or fake threat that turns out to be the V-01. Suspense is used to create anticipation and comedy, not to change the genre.

The humour may have an adult-leaning, irreverent edge through occasional strong profanity, blunt reactions and dark workplace humour. Keep it non-sexual. Do not use slurs, sexual jokes, graphic injury jokes or humour that targets real-world protected groups. Swearing is character-driven, not a substitute for writing.

## 2. Character-specific comedy

Every character must have a different comic rhythm. Do not make the whole squad sound like one comedian.

| Character | Comedy / fear style | How it should play |
|---|---|---|
| Rook (player) | Mostly player-led; brief authored responses only when essential | Do not force constant quips onto the player. Let the player's silence or action carry the moment. |
| Anchor | Dry, exhausted authority; tries to keep everyone professional | Delivers short deadpan corrections. Occasionally admits the situation is unsettling without losing leadership. |
| Patch | Sharp, practical, empathetic; reacts honestly when startled | Complains about the team's noise, checks that everyone is okay, then makes a very dry observation. |
| Echo | Understated, analytical and unintentionally funny | Identifies the mundane source of a scare. Her calm explanation can make the others feel even sillier. |
| Tally | The main chatterbox; confident until something actually makes a noise | Swears first, jokes second, denies being scared third. Can overstate how calm they are. |
| Hollis | Local, matter-of-fact, knows the forest better than the squad | Gives ordinary explanations for woodland sounds and quietly enjoys watching trained operators get spooked. |
| Lamplight | Professional radio handler with occasional dry replies | Does not become a comedian, but can deliver one perfectly timed response over comms. |
| Sorn | Controlled and severe | Do not turn the antagonist into a clown. His occasional exasperation is enough. |

## 3. Profanity and joke frequency

- Use stronger language sparingly at moments of genuine surprise, frustration or relief: examples include “What the fuck was that?”, “Oh, for fuck's sake” and “This place is a complete mess.”
- Most dialogue should remain natural and understandable without profanity.
- Do not insert a joke into every exchange. A useful rhythm is one short humorous beat after a tense sequence, then allow the scene to breathe.
- Let characters interrupt themselves, whisper, pause, speak over one another briefly or regret a comment, but never overlap important mission information.
- A joke should reveal personality, strengthen squad relationships or release tension. If it does none of those things, remove it.
- Keep the plot's serious human consequences out of the punchline. The squad may joke about a broken door, a terrible building, a strange noise or their own nerves; they should not mock the victims of the Northline attacks.

## 4. Environmental soundscape and scare language

Treat sound as a character in every location. Use layered, spatial, distance-aware audio with occlusion and restrained random variation. The player should sometimes hear something before seeing its mundane source.

### Forest — Green Silence

Sound palette:
- wind moving through upper canopy and ground foliage at different distances;
- isolated bird calls and insect beds, with gaps in the ambience;
- distant ridge-hound-like wildlife howls across the valley, answered by a second call from farther away;
- occasional low animal chuff or warning growl from beyond the brush, never close enough to become an attack encounter;
- a branch snapping, leaves shaking, a small animal darting away, and the sudden return of quiet;
- loose trail markers or a hanging cable tapping in gusts.

Staging:
1. Begin with a normal woodland ambience.
2. Let a distant howl travel across the stereo field and be answered from another direction.
3. A nearer rustle makes the squad stop. Tally asks what the noise was; Anchor listens; Echo identifies that the sound moved away rather than toward them.
4. Hollis explains that the ridge animals call to one another at dusk. The squad is embarrassed, but the forest remains alive and atmospheric.
5. Later, a single low growl and a rapid rustle make Tally briefly abandon the tough act. It is wildlife leaving the trail, not an enemy or a boss.

Do not make wildlife attack the player or force a combat encounter. Do not make the calls sound like a supernatural creature. Keep their source plausible and mostly off-screen.

### Perimeter — Dead Ground

Sound palette:
- chain-link fence singing in the wind;
- a loose warning sign tapping a metal post;
- a distant generator struggling under load;
- an old warning horn that gives one weak, broken note;
- a radio squelch or PA speaker feedback that momentarily resembles a voice.

Staging:
- The first apparent footsteps are the loose sign tapping at irregular intervals.
- Tally mistakes the horn for a voice. Echo explains the sound; Anchor tells the squad to stop interrogating the fence.
- Use one short pause where the team listens, then resumes once the sound's source is understood.

### Facility — Hard Entry / The Paper War

Sound palette:
- ventilation fans with a low, uneven growl;
- a pressure door's heavy servo and seal release;
- a PA speaker that clips the end of an announcement;
- pipes ticking as the building changes temperature;
- a distant trolley wheel or loose panel creating a short rolling noise;
- electrical relays and fluorescent fixtures with restrained hum/flicker.

Staging:
- A ventilation duct produces a low growl. Tally asks whether the building is breathing. Echo identifies the fan housing.
- A public-address chime begins and cuts off halfway. The squad pauses; the system resumes with a routine maintenance announcement.
- Never use these sounds to communicate real paranormal activity. They are building ambience and occasional false alarms.

### Bunker — Below the Line

Sound palette:
- slow pressure-door groans;
- water pipes knocking at different intervals;
- ventilation shafts carrying distant wind;
- a far-off metal impact whose echo takes a long time to decay;
- the bunker lift's cable and motor sounds;
- radio noise that briefly masks a word, but never hides a critical objective.

Staging:
- A door seal releases with a deep groan just as Tally is speaking. Tally stops mid-sentence.
- Patch asks whether the bunker always sounds like that. Venn answers that it does, then adds that he has stopped noticing it—which does not reassure anyone.
- A pipe knocks behind a wall. The squad pauses. Echo finds the pipe route on a service diagram and Tally calls it the building's “terrible percussion section.”
- Fear should be visible in body language: a glance into a dark side room, a hand raised to pause, a tighter formation, a quick check with a teammate. Do not make trained operators scream or run around randomly.

### Lower basement — The Source Vault

Sound palette:
- archive cooling fans that create a layered, almost breathing hum;
- a loose cable or suspended tag tapping metal at long intervals;
- old storage equipment starting up with a descending motor whine;
- a distant speaker repeating a clipped fragment of an old maintenance message;
- deep room reverb and a subtle low-frequency mechanical vibration.

Staging:
- The sound feels more oppressive because the space is larger and quieter, not because something supernatural is present.
- A repeating tap seems to follow the team. They discover it is a hanging archive tag moving in the airflow.
- An old speaker plays the fragment “...please remain...” before cutting out. Tally says the building has terrible customer service.
- The Source Vault must still read as a real archive and important evidence location, not a haunted maze.

### VICTUS chamber — V-01

The approach may build expectation with distant low machinery, a slowing fan and a heavy door. As the door opens, cut the ambience back to a quiet room tone. The V-01 dummy is completely motionless.

The joke lands because the squad has spent the campaign reacting to strange sounds and expecting a major secret. The final room is anticlimactically ordinary. Do not make the dummy move, blink, speak, turn its head, trigger a scare or become a threat. Its stillness is the point.

These are example lines and timing references, not a requirement to play every line. Adapt lines to the current dialogue scheduler, character state and mission context. Avoid repeating a line on every playthrough.

## 6. Performance, animation and interaction

Fear and humour should be visible, not just subtitles.

- On a sudden distant sound, nearby squad members may briefly stop, turn their head/torso toward its source and hold for a short listen.
- Give each operator a different reaction: Anchor raises a controlled hand signal; Patch glances toward the nearest teammate; Echo quietly tracks the source; Tally startles and then pretends not to have.
- Use short, bounded pauses. Never leave the squad stuck in a fear animation or block player control for a trivial sound.
- Use natural gaze, spacing and turn-taking. Characters should look toward the sound source and then at the teammate who comments.
- A character who makes a joke should receive a listener reaction: a look, eye-roll, brief exhale, head shake or small grin.
- Keep Rook's view first-person. Reactions from companions should happen in the world; do not switch to third-person gameplay.
- Do not play a joke line if a critical objective, enemy alert or Venn's important dialogue is active.
- Respect audio cooldowns, random selection and saved dialogue state so jokes do not repeat unnaturally.
- Provide a low-intensity ambience mix option if accessibility settings require it; never make a core objective dependent on hearing a subtle sound.

## 7. Audio implementation requirements

- Author sounds as spatial sources with plausible placement, distance falloff and occlusion where VICTUS supports them.
- Use different samples and timing variations for repeated fence taps, pipe knocks, animal calls and mechanical noises.
- Avoid stacking multiple loud scare sounds. The goal is atmosphere and comic tension, not startling the player every minute.
- Use silence deliberately before and after key reveals.
- The source of a false scare should be discoverable through sight, dialogue, an environmental clue or a later contextual reveal.
- Keep animal calls distant and non-hostile. Do not add animal attack AI or force the player to fight wildlife.
- Dialogue must remain intelligible. Important lines must never be buried under howling, machinery or radio static.
- Test ambience across phone speakers and headphones; low-frequency sounds that disappear on a phone should have a subtle mid-frequency component rather than simply being made louder.
- Profile concurrent audio voices and looping sources on the target device. Use finite variation sets and sensible voice limits.
- Record provenance/licensing for every animal, ambience, machinery and foley asset. Prefer original recordings, generated original sound or clearly redistributable assets.

## 8. Frequency and pacing rules

- A short environmental tension beat every few minutes is enough; not every corridor needs a noise.
- Use the strongest false-scare sequence in the forest, a couple of callbacks in the facility/bunker, and the most atmospheric sound bed in the lower basement.
- Place comedy after the scare, not on top of it. Allow a beat of silence, then let one character react.
- Never interrupt the plot's core evidence discoveries with optional banter.
- Escalate the squad's familiarity: early reactions are genuine uncertainty, later reactions are more self-aware, and the V-01 reveal pays off the accumulated expectation.
- Preserve the seriousness of the Northline crisis, Venn's detention and the conspiracy. Humour is a character layer, not a replacement for stakes.

## 9. Acceptance checklist

A campaign tone pass is complete only when:
- every main squad member has a recognisable and distinct comic voice;
- profanity is occasional, character-led and never used as filler;
- fear reactions are visible and brief, with player control preserved;
- the forest contains distant, plausible wildlife calls and at least one harmless false scare;
- the facility, bunker and lower basement have distinct unsettling mechanical sound identities;
- several scary-sounding events receive mundane explanations without becoming repetitive;
- jokes never obscure objectives, evidence or important dialogue;
- the V-01 dummy remains completely inert and the final reveal is funny rather than frightening;
- dialogue, subtitles, facial performance, gaze and audio cues are synchronized;
- ambience is tested on the target hardware where available and does not exceed the measured audio/performance budget.
